Negative results are systematically suppressed at a documented cost exceeding $50 billion annually. Patients — the source of all trial value — receive zero compensation for their health data and retain no ownership rights after enrollment. And cross-institutional collaboration, which would dramatically accelerate drug discovery, is legally blocked because no technical mechanism exists to analyze data from multiple hospitals without first exposing it.
TrialVault is the first clinical trial infrastructure platform built entirely on Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE). The platform enables computation on encrypted patient data: hospitals collaborate without sharing records, pharma companies match against patient cohorts without learning who they are, and trial results are committed to an immutable on-chain record before pharma ever sees the outcomes. Selective suppression becomes cryptographically impossible.
The platform consists of six deeply integrated modules: a Patient Data Vault (self-custody encrypted health data with ERC-721 ownership), a Trial Matching Engine (blind eligibility evaluation returning only aggregate counts), a Data Licensing Marketplace (token-gated patient earnings with atomic swaps), a Cross-Hospital FHE Analysis Layer (multi-party computation with differential privacy), a Result Integrity Layer (Merkle commitment and on-chain verification), and an Adverse Event Reporting module (threshold-triggered encrypted safety signals).
TrialVault is not a data platform with a privacy feature added. It is privacy-native from the first line of code. FHE is not a feature — it is the architecture. The platform is blind by design: it cannot read patient data even if it wanted to.

2. The Industry Problem
2.1 The Scale of the Crisis
Clinical trials are the mandatory pathway from scientific discovery to patient medicine. Every drug that reaches a pharmacy shelf has passed through years of clinical trial evaluation. That evaluation process is failing — not at the margins, but structurally and at scale.
The numbers are staggering and well-documented:
	•	85% of clinical trials fail to meet their patient recruitment targets on time. The average delay is 7 months per trial. For a Phase III trial, a single month of delay costs the sponsor between $600,000 and $8 million in delayed drug revenue, plus the compounding costs of trial extension.
	•	The average cost to recruit a single patient into a clinical trial is $41,000 — a figure that has risen 35% in the past decade and shows no sign of stabilizing. This cost exists because hospitals recruit in isolation, cannot share cohort data across institutions, and must resort to expensive outreach and site expansion.
	•	Approximately 50% of trial sites fail to recruit their target patient count. Under-enrolled trials produce statistically underpowered results — findings that cannot be confidently generalized to the patient population and may require repeat trials.
	•	The global clinical trial management market was valued at $54.2 billion in 2024, growing at 5.8% CAGR toward $85 billion by 2032. The majority of this value creation is consumed by recruitment delays, compliance overhead, and data management complexity — all of which TrialVault's FHE architecture directly addresses.

2.2 The Result Suppression Problem
Perhaps the most damaging and least discussed failure of the clinical trial system is the systematic, documented suppression of negative results. When a pharmaceutical company funds a trial that produces unfavorable outcomes, the institutional and financial incentives strongly favor non-publication.
A landmark analysis published in PLOS Medicine examined 12,000 clinical trial registrations and found industry-sponsored trials were 60% less likely to report negative results than independently funded studies. The estimated economic cost of this information asymmetry to global healthcare systems exceeds $50 billion annually — in drugs prescribed that were shown ineffective in hidden trials.
This is not a problem of individual bad actors. It is a structural problem: there is no mechanism to prove that a trial's published results match the original dataset. ClinicalTrials.gov requires registration and result posting, but cannot verify whether the posted results reflect the complete original data or a selectively filtered version. Pharmaceutical companies may legally change primary endpoints post-hoc, exclude unfavorable subgroups, and choose not to publish at all.
The consequence is real: patients receive treatments that were privately known to be ineffective, while more effective alternatives are delayed because the failed trial's negative data was never shared with the scientific community.

2.3 The Collaboration Barrier
Multi-institutional clinical research dramatically improves trial quality and statistical power. A trial drawing from five hospitals with 1,000 total patients is systematically more robust than five separate trials of 200 patients each. Yet cross-institutional collaboration remains the exception rather than the norm — not because of unwillingness, but because of technical and legal impossibility.
To analyze patient data across institutions, you must currently choose one of two approaches:
	•	Centralized data controller: All institutions transfer patient records to a single trusted repository. This creates a single point of failure, requires a Data Processing Agreement in every jurisdiction, generates ongoing GDPR and HIPAA compliance obligations, and exposes all data if the controller is breached.
	•	Federated learning: Train machine learning models locally at each institution; share only model gradient updates. This approach is vulnerable to gradient inversion attacks — a well-documented technique demonstrated by Zhu et al. (2019) and further refined by multiple groups since, by which an adversary can reconstruct individual patient records from shared gradient updates. This means federated learning is probabilistically private, not cryptographically private.
FHE is the only approach that provides mathematically unconditional privacy during computation. When data is encrypted under an FHE scheme, even the party running the computation cannot learn anything about the plaintext — this is proven cryptographically, not assumed operationally.

2.4 The Patient Data Ownership Gap
Every data point in every clinical trial was produced by a human patient who consented to participate. Those patients are the origin of all value in the clinical trial ecosystem. A successful Phase III trial can unlock a drug worth billions in annual revenue. The patient who provided the critical data point receives nothing: no compensation, no notification of results, no ability to revoke their data from future derivative analyses, and no transparency about how their health information has been used.
This is not merely an ethical concern — it is a practical recruitment barrier with measurable impact. Surveys consistently identify privacy concerns and lack of perceived personal benefit as among the top three reasons patients decline clinical trial enrollment. A platform that structurally guarantees patient data sovereignty, cryptographically prevents unauthorized access, and pays patients token royalties for each licensed use of their data directly addresses both barriers simultaneously.
Furthermore, the emerging EU Health Data Space regulation and equivalent frameworks in the UK, Canada, and Australia are moving toward requiring patient data portability and compensation rights. TrialVault's architecture is built for this regulatory direction — it will be compliant before these regulations become mandatory.

4. Fully Homomorphic Encryption: Technical Foundation
4.1 What FHE Is
Fully Homomorphic Encryption is a family of cryptographic schemes that allow arbitrary computation to be performed directly on encrypted data (ciphertext), producing an encrypted result that, when decrypted with the correct key, equals the result of performing the same computation on the original plaintext. The word 'fully' distinguishes FHE from 'somewhat' or 'leveled' homomorphic schemes that can only perform a limited number of operations before the ciphertext becomes too noisy to decrypt correctly.
The mathematical foundation of modern FHE is the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem, introduced by Regev (2005) and later extended by Brakerski, Gentry, Vaikuntanathan and others into practical schemes. The TFHE scheme used by Fhenix (Chillotti et al., 2016) is particularly suited to boolean circuits — making it efficient for comparison operations like the eligibility checks central to trial matching.

5. Core Protocol Modules
TrialVault consists of six smart contract modules, each addressing a distinct and documented failure mode in the existing clinical trial ecosystem. All modules are deployed as Solidity contracts on the Fhenix network and interact through a central TrialVaultRegistry.sol contract that maintains cross-module state and access control.
5.1 Module 1: Patient Data Vault
5.1.1 Problem Addressed
Patients have no mechanism to store health data in a sovereign, portable, privacy-preserving format. Every existing system — hospital EHR, health app, research database — is a silo controlled by the institution, not the patient.
5.1.2 Technical Architecture
The Patient Data Vault is an ERC-721 NFT contract with embedded FHE ciphertext storage. Each patient's health data is encrypted client-side using the fhevmjs SDK before any network transmission. The encrypted data blob is stored on IPFS; the IPFS CID and encrypted metadata fields are committed on-chain.
5.1.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Patient connects wallet. fhevmjs SDK generates a session FHE keypair. Private key stored encrypted in localStorage, protected by a key derived from the patient's wallet signing key via HKDF.
	•	Health records uploaded in browser. Parser extracts structured fields: age (integer), ICD-10 diagnosis codes (8-bit integers), LOINC lab values (scaled 32-bit integers), medication codes.
	•	Client-side encryption: encAge = await instance.encrypt32(age); encDiagnosis = await instance.encrypt8(icd10AsInt); encLab1 = await instance.encrypt32(labValue * 100) — all in browser WASM.
	•	Encrypted blob uploaded to IPFS. CID returned. Patient signs a transaction calling PatientVault.registerData(cid, encAge, encDiagnosis, encLab1, encLab2).
	•	Contract mints ERC-721 Data NFT to patient's wallet. Token URI points to IPFS CID. Patient configures licensing preferences: minimum price (TVAULT), allowed categories bitmap, geographic whitelist.
5.1.4 Smart Contract Interface
interface IPatientVault {
  // Register encrypted health data and mint Data NFT
  function registerData(
    string calldata ipfsCid,
    euint8  encryptedDiagnosisCode,
    euint32 encryptedAge,

  ) external;

  // Revoke data access from a specific licensee
  function revokeAccess(uint256 tokenId, address licensee) external;

  event DataRegistered(address indexed patient, uint256 tokenId, string ipfsCid);
  event AccessRevoked(uint256 indexed tokenId, address indexed licensee);
}

5.2 Module 2: Trial Matching Engine
5.2.1 Problem Addressed
Patient recruitment costs $41,000 per patient because hospitals recruit in isolation. No mechanism exists to match pharma eligibility criteria against a multi-hospital patient pool without exposing patient records or criteria to the other party.
5.2.2 Technical Architecture
The Trial Matching Engine evaluates encrypted eligibility criteria against encrypted patient records using FHE operations. Neither pharma's criteria nor patient health data is ever in plaintext on-chain. The output for each patient is an encrypted ebool. The only decrypted output is an aggregate pool count.
5.2.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Pharma submits TrialMatcher.createTrial() with encrypted criteria: encMinAge, encMaxAge, encDiagnosisCode, encLabMin, encLabMax — all encrypted by the pharma portal before submission.
	•	TrialMatcher.runMatching(trialId) iterates over registered patient vaults. For each patient: ageOK = FHE.and(FHE.gte(p.encAge, criteria.minAge), FHE.lte(p.encAge, criteria.maxAge)); diagOK = FHE.eq(p.encDiagnosis, criteria.diagCode); labOK = FHE.and(FHE.gte(p.encLab, criteria.labMin), FHE.lte(p.encLab, criteria.labMax)); fullMatch = FHE.and(FHE.and(ageOK, diagOK), labOK).
	•	Pool count: encCount = FHE.add(encCount, FHE.select(fullMatch, euint32(1), euint32(0))). After all patients: pharma calls TFHE.decrypt(encCount) → receives integer (e.g. 312). No individual records exposed.
	•	For each fullMatch=true patient, contract emits ConsentRequest event to patient wallet. Patient reviews trial details off-chain. Consent is granted by calling grantConsent(trialId) — a separate voluntary transaction.
5.2.4 Smart Contract Interface
interface ITrialMatcher {

  ) external returns (uint256 trialId);

  function getPoolCount(uint256 trialId) external returns (uint32);

5.3 Module 3: Data Licensing Marketplace
5.3.1 Problem Addressed
Patients contribute health data and earn nothing. Pharma companies pay millions in infrastructure costs to access patient pools. There is no direct economic relationship between data producers (patients) and data consumers (pharma) — all value is captured by institutional intermediaries.
5.3.2 Technical Architecture
The Licensing Marketplace is an escrow contract implementing atomic swaps between encrypted key fragments and TVAULT token payments. Atomicity is enforced by the smart contract: payment releases only when a valid key fragment is received, and the key fragment is released only when payment is confirmed. No trust between patient and pharma is required.
5.3.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Patient lists dataset: LicenseMarket.listDataset(tokenId, minPriceWei, categoryBitmap). Listing emits DatasetListed event indexed by The Graph.
	•	Pharma requests license: LicenseMarket.requestLicense(datasetId, purpose), attaching required TVAULT payment. Tokens held in contract escrow. LicenseRequested emitted to patient.
	•	Patient reviews purpose. If approved, signs transaction calling releaseKeyFragment(licenseId, encryptedKeyFragment). Key fragment is re-encrypted specifically for pharma's FHE public key.
	•	Contract atomically: releases escrowed TVAULT to patient wallet; stores key fragment accessible only to pharma's wallet; records license on-chain with Merkle commitment.
	•	Pharma downloads encrypted blob from IPFS, uses key fragment to decrypt the licensed fields only. Unlicensed fields remain encrypted and inaccessible.
	•	Ongoing royalties: if pharma uses data in derivative analyses, ResearchDerivative.sol tracks usage and enforces royalty distributions configured at license creation.

5.4 Module 4: Cross-Hospital Encrypted Analysis
5.4.1 Problem Addressed
Multi-hospital clinical trials require data aggregation across independent institutions. Existing approaches require either centralizing patient records (single point of failure) or federated learning (gradient inversion vulnerabilities). Neither provides a cryptographic privacy guarantee.
5.4.2 Technical Architecture
MultiHospitalTrial.sol implements FHE-based multi-party computation. Each hospital encrypts its patient cohort under a shared trial public key generated via a distributed key generation (DKG) ceremony. No single party holds the complete private key. Analysis functions (mean, variance, correlation) are evaluated on the combined ciphertext. Differential privacy noise is applied before decryption. Decryption requires a threshold signature (e.g. 2-of-3 hospital signatures).
5.4.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Trial coordinator deploys MultiHospitalTrial.sol specifying: participant hospital addresses, analysis type (MEAN/VARIANCE/CORRELATION/SURVIVAL), epsilon (differential privacy parameter ε ∈ {0.5, 1, 2}), and expected total cohort size.
	•	DKG ceremony: participating hospitals run a threshold key generation protocol off-chain (using TFHE-rs distributed key generation). The resulting shared public key is registered on-chain. No private key fragments are on-chain.
	•	Each hospital signs a multi-sig participation agreement stored as a hash on-chain. All hospitals must sign before data submission opens.
	•	Hospitals upload encrypted patient data in batches of 50 (gas management). Each record: tuple (encAge: euint32, encDiagnosis: euint8, encLab1: euint32, encLab2: euint32).
	•	runAnalysis() aggregates using FHE.add() across all uploaded records. For mean: encSum = sum of all encField values; encMean = FHE.div(encSum, euint32(totalPatients)). Differential privacy: encResult = FHE.add(encMean, sampleLaplaceNoise(epsilon)).
	•	Decryption: requestDecryption() requires ≥ threshold hospital signatures. Decrypted output: aggregate statistics only (mean, std dev, quartiles). No path to reconstruct individual records from aggregate output.
5.4.4 Smart Contract Interface
interface IMultiHospitalTrial {
  function deployTrial(
    address[] calldata hospitals,
    uint8 analysisType,           // 0=MEAN,1=VARIANCE,2=CORRELATION
    uint8 dpEpsilon,              // Differential privacy epsilon * 10
    bytes calldata sharedPubKey   // DKG output
  ) external returns (uint256 trialId);

  function signParticipation(uint256 trialId) external;
  function submitEncryptedCohort(uint256 trialId, euint32[] calldata encAges,
    euint8[] calldata encDiagnoses, euint32[] calldata encLabs) external;
  function runAnalysis(uint256 trialId) external;
  function requestDecryption(uint256 trialId, bytes calldata sig) external;

  event AnalysisComplete(uint256 indexed trialId, uint256 totalPatients);
  event DecryptionThresholdReached(uint256 indexed trialId);
}

5.5 Module 5: Result Integrity Layer
5.5.1 Problem Addressed
Pharma companies routinely suppress negative trial results. There is no current mechanism to prove that published results match the original dataset. ClinicalTrials.gov requires result posting but cannot cryptographically verify integrity. Post-hoc endpoint modification, selective population exclusion, and outright non-publication are documented practices with no technical remedy.
5.5.2 Technical Architecture
ResultIntegrity.sol implements a Merkle commitment scheme. Before any patient is enrolled, the trial protocol (statistical analysis plan, primary/secondary endpoints, inclusion/exclusion criteria) is hashed and committed on-chain. Before any analysis is run, the complete enrolled dataset is committed as a Merkle root. Published results include a Merkle proof linking the published findings to the committed root. Any third party can verify integrity on-chain in a single function call.
5.5.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Pre-enrollment: pharma calls commitProtocol(trialId, SHA256(SAP + endpoints + criteria)). This hash is immutable from this point.
	•	Enrollment complete: pharma calls commitDataset(trialId, merkleRoot) where merkleRoot = Merkle root of all enrolled patient records and all collected data points. This is committed before any statistical analysis is run.
	•	At each analysis checkpoint: commitCheckpoint(trialId, checkpointIndex, SHA256(checkpointData)). Block timestamp is stored immutably.
	•	Publication: publishResults(trialId, SHA256(results), merkleProofs[]). Contract verifies MerkleProof.verify(merkleProofs, merkleRoot, SHA256(results)). Fails if results don't match committed data.
	•	Verification: any regulator, journal, or third party calls verifyIntegrity(trialId, resultsHash) → returns bool. A false return is publicly visible on-chain and constitutes provable evidence of data manipulation.
5.5.4 Smart Contract Interface
interface IResultIntegrity {
  function commitProtocol(uint256 trialId, bytes32 protocolHash) external;
  function commitDataset(uint256 trialId, bytes32 merkleRoot) external;
  function commitCheckpoint(uint256 trialId, uint8 idx, bytes32 chkHash) external;
  function publishResults(uint256 trialId, bytes32 resultsHash,
    bytes32[] calldata proof) external;
  function verifyIntegrity(uint256 trialId, bytes32 resultsHash)
    external view returns (bool);

  event ProtocolCommitted(uint256 indexed trialId, bytes32 hash, uint256 timestamp);
  event ResultsPublished(uint256 indexed trialId, bytes32 resultsHash, bool verified);
}

5.6 Module 6: Adverse Event Reporting
5.6.1 Problem Addressed
Trial sponsors have historically suppressed or delayed adverse event reporting. In the Vioxx scandal, Merck was found to have withheld adverse cardiovascular event data for years. Current systems rely on sponsor self-reporting to regulatory databases. There is no mechanism for patients to anonymously report side effects that cannot be suppressed by the trial sponsor.
5.6.2 Technical Architecture
EventReporter.sol maintains an encrypted running aggregate of adverse event reports. Patients submit via burner addresses funded through a privacy-preserving relayer — decoupling the report from the patient's primary wallet. The aggregate severity score is compared to an encrypted threshold using FHE.gte(). When the threshold is breached, an uncensorable on-chain alert fires automatically. No party can prevent the alert because it is triggered by smart contract logic, not by human decision.
5.6.3 Detailed Workflow
	•	Patient experiences side effect. Patient portal generates a fresh burner address, funds it via a mixer contract (or a privacy-preserving relayer). Patient submits from burner: EventReporter.submitEvent(trialId, encEventCode, encSeverity).
	•	Contract updates: totalEvents = FHE.add(totalEvents, euint32(1)); totalSeverity = FHE.add(totalSeverity, encSeverity). Running aggregates maintained fully encrypted.
	•	At each batch (every 100 submissions or 24 hours): thresholdBreached = FHE.gte(totalSeverity, encryptedThreshold). If true: contract emits SafetyAlert(trialId, blockNumber) — public, immutable, uncensorable.
	•	Regulatory query: threshold-signed decryption releases aggregate event counts (not individual records) to authorized regulators.

8.2 Why Existing Solutions Cannot Close the Gap
	•	ClinicalTrials.gov is a registration database. It verifies trial registration but cannot enforce result integrity. Selective disclosure has no technical remedy under its architecture.
	•	Medidata and Veeva are centralized data management systems built on conventional databases. They require operators to trust platform security. A compromised admin or insider threat exposes all client trial data. There is no cryptographic privacy guarantee.
